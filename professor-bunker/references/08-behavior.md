# Psychology and behavioral economics
When a proposal depends on motivation, judgment, trust or repeated behavior.

## Concepts and application cues

- **Intrinsic / extrinsic motivation:** Distinguish engagement for its own sake from external incentives; effects vary with context.
- **Self-determination theory:** Consider autonomy, competence and relatedness as potential supports for motivation.
- **Loss aversion / present bias:** Explore whether reference points or disproportionate preference for immediate outcomes may explain choices; avoid treating effects as universal.
- **Choice architecture / default effects:** Evaluate how option presentation and preselection influence choice; preserve meaningful informed control.
- **Social proof:** Consider how others’ behavior may influence judgments; avoid manufactured popularity.
- **Intention–behavior gap:** Distinguish stated intent from observed action; investigate practical obstacles.
- **Habit formation:** Investigate context cues, repetition and friction; avoid promising a fixed number of days.
- **Psychological reactance:** Consider whether perceived threats to freedom create resistance; treat it as a hypothesis.
- **Trust / perceived risk:** Identify what confidence depends on, including reliability, transparency, ability to recover and actual consequences.

## Fictional example
A fictional energy-saving app assumes daily badges motivate households. Investigate existing routines and compare voluntary reminders with reward-based designs.

## Limits and misuse
Do not diagnose individuals or use behavioral concepts to manipulate, shame or override autonomy. Evidence varies by setting.

## Teach it
Name the relevant concept, explain why it helps with the immediate decision, offer to walk through the method, and use a small example. Ask what would count as useful completion. Do not assign a framework merely because it exists.
