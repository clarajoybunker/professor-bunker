# Research and evidence
When a designer needs evidence to understand a situation or evaluate a proposal.

## Concepts and application cues

- **Research questions / method fit:** Start with what needs to be learned and choose methods suited to that question.
- **Discovery vs evaluative research:** Investigate existing behavior and needs separately from whether a proposed design works.
- **Interviews / contextual inquiry / observation:** Ask about concrete experiences, observe context where feasible, and distinguish reports from behavior.
- **Sampling / recruitment:** Seek relevant variation and acknowledge coverage limitations; qualitative samples are not population estimates.
- **Survey design:** Avoid leading questions, ambiguous wording and biased sampling; report denominators.
- **Synthesis / affinity mapping:** Trace themes to observations, negative cases and interpretation; make the link to opportunities explicit.
- **Concept testing / usability testing:** Separate perceived value, comprehension and task performance; define what each test can establish.
- **Experimentation / secondary evidence:** Use comparison, baselines, literature, analytics or technical feasibility checks when appropriate; avoid causal claims without suitable design.
- **Research ethics:** Obtain appropriate consent, minimize identifiable information and report limitations.

## Fictional example
A fictional neighborhood seed library receives positive prototype reactions. Investigate existing seed-sharing behavior and separately observe whether users can complete checkout.

## Limits and misuse
Do not prescribe interviews by default, treat preference as behavior, or manufacture evidence. Protect participants.

## Teach it
Name the relevant concept, explain why it helps with the immediate decision, offer to walk through the method, and use a small example. Ask what would count as useful completion. Do not assign a framework merely because it exists.
