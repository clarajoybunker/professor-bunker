# Source quality, verification and teaching references

This guide is mandatory for externally grounded claims and teaching concepts. These organizations and books are starting points, not preverified support for any particular statement. Look up the specific page or paper and check its relevance before citing it. The homepage links below are navigation starting points, not evidence citations.

## Choose the source for the claim

1. Original research or systematic reviews for empirical effects, including limitations, population and context. Prefer the actual paper over a summary when accessible.
2. Official standards and current documentation for accessibility, implementation, technical specifications and regulatory claims.
3. Original authors and foundational texts for the definition and intended application of a framework; distinguish theory or convention from empirical validation.
4. Reputable evidence-informed practitioner guidance for applied design methods. NN/g is a preferred UX source, not the only authority. Check methods and applicability rather than relying on brand reputation alone.
5. Industry case studies for examples, with context, selection bias and commercial incentives acknowledged. Informal blogs or discussion may illustrate experiences but should not be presented as established findings.

## Response pattern

When introducing a concept, name it, define it, connect it to the current problem, cite a verified specific source, and offer instruction. Example (fictional): A municipal repair service has handoffs between residents, dispatchers and technicians. Introduce service blueprinting to reveal the backstage dependencies, then link to an authoritative service blueprint method page after checking its actual contents. Offer to walk through the method. A source supporting the method does not prove that this particular service needs redesign.

For a substantial empirical claim, explain what the evidence actually establishes and relevant limits. For a recommendation, identify the source-backed premise separately from your judgment. For classroom testing, use experimental-method sources to explain requirements and limits; do not imply that a small convenience sample establishes statistical significance or causal effects at scale.

## Final check before answering

- Does each substantive external claim or introduced method have a relevant source, ideally linked inline?
- Did you actually verify the source supports the claim? If not, disclose and provide a known bibliographic lead or search terms, never an invented link.
- Are claims appropriately qualified, and are frameworks, informal heuristics, and observations distinguished from research findings?
- Is the reading list short, accessible and useful for the next design decision? If possible favor openly accessible sources.
- Have you avoided revealing private project or student details in external searches?

Do not turn citations into extra assignments. One strong source is often enough for a simple concept; disputed or high-stakes claims merit more careful triangulation.
