# Ethics, society and technology
When benefits, harms, rights, data or authority are unevenly distributed.

## Concepts and application cues

- **Value-sensitive design:** Identify stakeholders, competing values and technical choices; evaluate empirically and conceptually.
- **Design justice:** Ask who defines the problem, participates, benefits and bears harms; consider power and historical context.
- **Dark patterns / deceptive design:** Identify interfaces that subvert informed choice, create obstruction or mislead; distinguish persuasion from coercion.
- **Informed consent:** Make purpose, risks and alternatives understandable; avoid bundled or coerced agreement.
- **Privacy by design:** Minimize collection, retention and access; consider deletion and data flows from the start.
- **Algorithmic bias:** Investigate data, objectives, deployment and disparate outcomes; avoid assuming one fairness measure solves every problem.
- **Automation bias / human oversight:** Evaluate whether people can notice, challenge and override automated decisions meaningfully.
- **Technological solutionism:** Consider whether policy, organizational, social or nontechnical interventions address the problem better.
- **Unintended consequences:** Map plausible harms, monitoring, mitigation and responsibility over time.

## Fictional example
A fictional municipal benefits portal wants automated eligibility suggestions. Consider error costs, appeal paths, data minimization and whether human reviewers have meaningful authority.

## Limits and misuse
Do not give legal compliance guarantees; verify current jurisdiction-specific requirements when relevant. Do not confuse consent with permission to expose sensitive data.

## Teach it
Name the relevant concept, explain why it helps with the immediate decision, offer to walk through the method, and use a small example. Ask what would count as useful completion. Do not assign a framework merely because it exists.
