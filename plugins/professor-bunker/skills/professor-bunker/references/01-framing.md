# Problem framing and critical thinking
When the problem, assumptions or causal explanation is unclear.

## Concepts and application cues

- **Problem framing / reframing:** Separate the situation, affected people, desired outcome and proposed intervention; reframe when the original question prematurely commits to a solution.
- **Challenging assumptions / assumption mapping:** Identify what must be true for a proposal to work; rank by consequence and uncertainty, then test the riskiest assumptions.
- **MECE:** Try non-overlapping, collectively covering categories when organizing a bounded question; acknowledge that lived experiences may overlap.
- **First principles:** Separate genuine constraints from conventions; rebuild alternatives from the underlying requirements.
- **Abstraction / specificity:** Move up to the underlying goal or down to a concrete behavior when the problem is too broad or narrow.
- **Root cause analysis / causal hypotheses:** Distinguish symptoms from possible causes; use evidence and competing explanations rather than assuming one root cause.
- **Hypothesis / counterfactual:** State what you expect to happen and compare with what might happen without the intervention.
- **Cognitive biases:** Consider anchoring, confirmation bias and sunk costs as possible explanations, not diagnoses of individuals.
- **Necessary vs sufficient conditions:** Ask whether a proposed change is required, enough on its own, both or neither.

## Fictional example
A fictional community tool-lending service assumes reminders will reduce late returns. Explore whether opening hours, transport or unclear rules better explain the behavior.

## Limits and misuse
Do not force neat categories on complex experiences, or treat a fishbone diagram or five whys as proof of causation.

## Teach it
Name the relevant concept, explain why it helps with the immediate decision, offer to walk through the method, and use a small example. Ask what would count as useful completion. Do not assign a framework merely because it exists.
